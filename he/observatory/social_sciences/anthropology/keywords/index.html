    Key Terms & Concepts — Anthropology¶

    A curated, scannable reference of important terms in anthropology. Hover a term to reveal its definition, aliases, and an external anchor. Terms are grouped by real sub-fields — never a General or Other catch-all.

    Cultural Anthropology¶

    • Culture (1)
    • Cultural relativism (2)
    • Ethnocentrism (3)
    • Emic and etic (4)
    • Enculturation (5)
    1. The system of shared beliefs, values, customs, behaviors, and artifacts that members of a society learn and use to interact with the world and one another. See also: Wikipedia.
    2. The principle that a person's beliefs and practices should be understood on the basis of that person's own culture rather than judged against the criteria of another. See also: Wikipedia.
    3. The tendency to evaluate other cultures according to the standards and customs of one's own. See also: Wikipedia.
    4. Two contrasting approaches to describing behavior — emic from within the culture's own conceptual framework, etic from the external framework of the observer. See also: Wikipedia.
    5. The process by which a person learns the norms, values, and behaviors appropriate to their culture. See also: Wikipedia.

    Biological (Physical) Anthropology¶

    • Biological anthropology (1)
    • Human evolution (2)
    • Paleoanthropology (3)
    • Primatology (4)
    • Hominini (5)
    1. The subfield studying the biological and behavioral aspects of humans, their extinct ancestors, and related non-human primates. Aliases: physical anthropology. See also: Wikipedia.
    2. The evolutionary process that led to the emergence of anatomically modern humans. See also: Wikipedia.
    3. The study of ancient humans and human ancestors through fossil and other physical remains. See also: Wikipedia.
    4. The scientific study of non-human primates. See also: Wikipedia.
    5. The taxonomic tribe that includes modern humans together with their extinct bipedal relatives. Aliases: hominins. See also: Wikipedia.

    Archaeology¶

    • Archaeology (1)
    • Excavation (2)
    • Stratigraphy (3)
    • Artifact (4)
    • Radiocarbon dating (5)
    1. The study of human activity through the recovery and analysis of material culture. See also: Wikipedia.
    2. The controlled recovery and recording of buried archaeological remains from the ground. See also: Wikipedia.
    3. The study of layered deposits (strata) to establish the relative chronological sequence of a site. See also: Wikipedia.
    4. An object made or modified by humans and recovered archaeologically, typically of cultural or historical interest. See also: Wikipedia.
    5. A method for determining the age of organic material by measuring its content of the radioactive isotope carbon-14. Aliases: carbon dating, carbon-14 dating. See also: Wikipedia.

    Linguistic Anthropology¶

    • Linguistic anthropology (1)
    • Linguistic relativity (2)
    • Language documentation (3)
    1. The subfield studying how language shapes and reflects social life and culture. See also: Wikipedia.
    2. The hypothesis that the structure of a language influences its speakers' worldview or cognition. Aliases: Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, Whorfianism. See also: Wikipedia.
    3. The systematic recording, analysis, and archiving of a language, especially an endangered one. Aliases: documentary linguistics. See also: Wikipedia.

    Ethnography & Fieldwork¶

    • Ethnography (1)
    • Participant observation (2)
    • Field research (3)
    • Thick description (4)
    • Reflexivity (5)
    1. The systematic study and written description of individual cultures based on firsthand fieldwork. See also: Wikipedia.
    2. A fieldwork method in which the researcher takes part in the daily life of the group being studied while observing it. See also: Wikipedia.
    3. Data collection carried out in a natural, real-world setting rather than in a laboratory. Aliases: fieldwork. See also: Wikipedia.
    4. An account of human behavior that explains not only the behavior but its context, so that it becomes meaningful to an outsider. See also: Wikipedia.
    5. The practice of examining how the researcher's own position and actions affect the research process and its findings. See also: Wikipedia.

    Kinship & Social Organization¶

    • Kinship (1)
    • Lineage (2)
    • Clan (3)
    • Exogamy (4)
    • Rite of passage (5)
    1. The web of social relationships based on descent, marriage, and adoption that forms a central organizing principle in many societies. See also: Wikipedia.
    2. A kin group whose members trace descent from a common ancestor through a known genealogical line. See also: Wikipedia.
    3. A group of people united by perceived kinship and descent, often from a common ancestor. See also: Wikipedia.
    4. The social norm or custom of marrying outside one's own social group. See also: Wikipedia.
    5. A ritual marking a person's transition from one social status to another. See also: Wikipedia.
