# Key Terms & Concepts — Engineering

A curated, scannable reference of important terms in engineering. Hover a term to reveal its
definition, aliases, and an external anchor. Terms are grouped by real engineering
disciplines — never a `General` or `Other` catch-all.

## Design & Systems Engineering

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Systems engineering (1)
- Factor of safety (2)
- Finite element method (3)
- Computer-aided design (4)

</div>

1.  An interdisciplinary field focused on the design, integration, and management of complex systems across their whole life cycle. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_engineering).
2.  The ratio of a component's load-carrying capacity to the load it is actually expected to bear, expressing how much stronger a design is than it strictly needs to be. **Aliases:** safety factor. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_of_safety).
3.  A numerical method that subdivides a large problem into smaller, simpler parts (finite elements) to approximate solutions to structural, thermal, and fluid problems. **Aliases:** FEM, finite element analysis. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element_method).
4.  The use of computers to create, modify, analyze, and document a design. **Aliases:** CAD. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design).

## Mechanical Engineering

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Mechanical engineering (1)
- Thermodynamics (2)
- Stress (3)
- Strain (4)
- Fatigue (5)

</div>

1.  The engineering discipline concerned with the design, analysis, and manufacture of mechanical systems, applying principles of physics and materials science. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_engineering).
2.  The branch of physics dealing with heat, work, temperature, and their relation to energy and entropy — central to engines, refrigeration, and power generation. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics).
3.  The internal forces that neighbouring particles of a material exert on each other, expressed as force per unit area. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(mechanics)).
4.  The measure of a material's deformation, expressed as the relative change in its shape or size under load. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_(mechanics)).
5.  The weakening of a material caused by repeatedly applied loads, which can cause failure well below the material's static strength. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_(material)).

## Electrical & Electronics Engineering

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Electrical engineering (1)
- Ohm's law (2)
- Alternating current (3)
- Transistor (4)

</div>

1.  The engineering discipline concerned with the study and application of electricity, electronics, and electromagnetism. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering).
2.  The principle that the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm%27s_law).
3.  An electric current that periodically reverses direction, in contrast to direct current; the form in which electric power is delivered to homes and businesses. **Aliases:** AC. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current).
4.  A semiconductor device used to amplify or switch electrical signals and power — the fundamental building block of modern electronics. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor).

## Civil & Structural Engineering

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Civil engineering (1)
- Structural engineering (2)
- Reinforced concrete (3)
- Truss (4)

</div>

1.  The engineering discipline dealing with the design, construction, and maintenance of the built environment — roads, bridges, dams, and buildings. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering).
2.  A sub-discipline of civil engineering focused on the framework of structures and on designing them to safely resist the loads they must bear. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineering).
3.  A composite material in which concrete's low tensile strength is compensated by embedding reinforcement (typically steel bars) before the concrete sets. **Aliases:** ferroconcrete. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete).
4.  A structure of connected members, usually forming triangular units, that behaves as a rigid body and efficiently carries loads. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truss).

## Chemical Engineering

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Chemical engineering (1)
- Unit operation (2)
- Distillation (3)
- Catalysis (4)

</div>

1.  The engineering discipline that applies chemistry, physics, biology, and mathematics to design and operate processes that convert raw materials into useful products. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering).
2.  A basic step in a process that involves a physical change or transformation, such as separation, mixing, or heat transfer. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_operation).
3.  The separation of components of a liquid mixture by selective evaporation and condensation, exploiting differences in volatility. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation).
4.  The increase in the rate of a chemical reaction by adding a substance (a catalyst) that is not consumed by the reaction. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis).

## Materials Science & Engineering

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Materials science (1)
- Alloy (2)
- Composite material (3)
- Young's modulus (4)

</div>

1.  The interdisciplinary field that studies the relationship between the structure of materials and their properties, and applies it to design and manufacture. **Aliases:** materials engineering. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science).
2.  A material made by combining a metal with one or more other elements to improve properties such as strength, hardness, or corrosion resistance. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy).
3.  A material made from two or more constituent materials with different properties that, combined, produce characteristics distinct from the individual components. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_material).
4.  A mechanical property measuring the stiffness of a solid material — the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the elastic region. **Aliases:** elastic modulus, modulus of elasticity. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%27s_modulus).

## Control Systems

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Control theory (1)
- Feedback (2)
- PID controller (3)
- Transfer function (4)

</div>

1.  A field of control engineering and applied mathematics dealing with the control of dynamical systems to achieve a desired behaviour. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_theory).
2.  The process by which a portion of a system's output is returned to its input to influence subsequent behaviour, enabling self-regulation. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback).
3.  A control-loop mechanism that continuously computes an error value and applies proportional, integral, and derivative corrections. **Aliases:** proportional–integral–derivative controller. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PID_controller).
4.  A mathematical representation that relates the output of a system to its input, used to model and analyze linear time-invariant systems. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_function).
