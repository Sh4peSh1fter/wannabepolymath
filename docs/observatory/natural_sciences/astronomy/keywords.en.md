# Key Terms & Concepts — Astronomy

A curated, scannable reference of important terms in astronomy. Hover a term to reveal its
definition, aliases, and an external anchor. Terms are grouped by real sub-topics — never a
`General` or `Other` catch-all.

## Solar System

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Planet (1)
- Asteroid (2)
- Comet (3)
- Astronomical unit (4)
- Kuiper belt (5)
- Oort cloud (6)

</div>

1.  A large body orbiting a star, massive enough to be rounded by its own gravity and to have cleared its orbital neighbourhood of other objects. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet).
2.  A rocky, airless remnant of the early Solar System, most orbiting the Sun in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. **Aliases:** minor planet. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid).
3.  A small icy body that releases gas and dust to form a glowing coma and tail when it approaches the Sun. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet).
4.  A unit of length equal to the mean Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million kilometres. **Aliases:** AU. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit).
5.  A ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune's orbit, the source of many short-period comets. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuiper_belt).
6.  A hypothesized spherical shell of icy objects at the outer edge of the Solar System, thought to be the source of long-period comets. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oort_cloud).

## Stars & Stellar Evolution

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Star (1)
- Nuclear fusion (2)
- Main sequence (3)
- Nebula (4)
- Supernova (5)
- White dwarf (6)
- Neutron star (7)
- Black hole (8)
- Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (9)

</div>

1.  A luminous sphere of plasma held together by its own gravity, generating energy by nuclear fusion in its core. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star).
2.  The process in which atomic nuclei combine to form heavier nuclei, releasing the energy that powers stars. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion).
3.  The continuous band on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram where stars spend most of their lives fusing hydrogen into helium. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence).
4.  An interstellar cloud of gas and dust, often a site of star formation or the remnant of a dying star. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula).
5.  A powerful stellar explosion marking the end of a massive star's life or the runaway ignition of a white dwarf. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova).
6.  The dense, cooling core left behind by a low- to medium-mass star after it exhausts its nuclear fuel. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf).
7.  An extremely dense collapsed core of a massive star, composed almost entirely of neutrons. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star).
8.  A region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole).
9.  A scatter plot of stars showing the relationship between their luminosity and surface temperature. **Aliases:** H–R diagram. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertzsprung%E2%80%93Russell_diagram).

## Galaxies & Deep-Sky Objects

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Galaxy (1)
- Milky Way (2)
- Quasar (3)
- Active galactic nucleus (4)
- Dark matter (5)

</div>

1.  A gravitationally bound system of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter, ranging from dwarfs to giants of trillions of stars. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy).
2.  The barred spiral galaxy that contains the Solar System. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way).
3.  An extremely luminous active galactic nucleus powered by gas falling onto a supermassive black hole. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar).
4.  A compact, highly luminous region at the centre of a galaxy whose emission is dominated by accretion onto a supermassive black hole. **Aliases:** AGN. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_galactic_nucleus).
5.  A form of matter that does not emit or absorb light but whose gravitational effects are inferred from the motion of galaxies. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter).

## Cosmology

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Big Bang (1)
- Cosmic microwave background (2)
- Redshift (3)
- Hubble's law (4)
- Dark energy (5)

</div>

1.  The prevailing cosmological model describing the expansion of the universe from an early hot, dense state. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang).
2.  Faint microwave radiation filling the universe, the relic thermal radiation left over from the early universe. **Aliases:** CMB. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background).
3.  The increase in the wavelength of light from an object moving away from the observer, key evidence for cosmic expansion. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift).
4.  The observation that galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance, evidence that the universe is expanding. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble%27s_law).
5.  A hypothesized form of energy driving the accelerating expansion of the universe. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy).

## Observational Astronomy

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Telescope (1)
- Astronomical spectroscopy (2)
- Apparent magnitude (3)
- Light-year (4)
- Parsec (5)
- Radio astronomy (6)

</div>

1.  An instrument that gathers and focuses electromagnetic radiation to observe distant objects. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope).
2.  The study of the spectra of astronomical objects to determine their composition, temperature, motion, and other properties. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_spectroscopy).
3.  A measure of the brightness of a celestial object as seen from Earth, on a reversed logarithmic scale. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_magnitude).
4.  A unit of distance equal to how far light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year).
5.  A unit of distance equal to about 3.26 light-years, defined by stellar parallax. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec).
6.  The study of celestial objects at radio wavelengths of the electromagnetic spectrum. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_astronomy).

## Celestial Mechanics

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Orbit (1)
- Kepler's laws of planetary motion (2)
- Newton's law of universal gravitation (3)
- Escape velocity (4)
- Lagrange point (5)

</div>

1.  The curved, gravitationally bound path of one object around another, such as a planet around a star. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit).
2.  Three laws describing the elliptical orbits of planets around the Sun and the relationships governing their motion. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_laws_of_planetary_motion).
3.  The law stating that every mass attracts every other mass with a force proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_law_of_universal_gravitation).
4.  The minimum speed an object needs to break free of a body's gravitational pull without further propulsion. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_velocity).
5.  A point in a two-body system where a small object can maintain a stable position relative to the two large bodies. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_point).
