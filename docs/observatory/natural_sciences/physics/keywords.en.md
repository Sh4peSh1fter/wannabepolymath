# Key Terms & Concepts — Physics

A curated, scannable reference of important terms in physics. Hover a term to reveal its
definition, aliases, and an external anchor. Terms are grouped by real sub-fields — never a
`General` or `Other` catch-all.

## Classical Mechanics

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Newton's laws of motion (1)
- Momentum (2)
- Energy (3)
- Young–Laplace equation (4)

</div>

1.  Three laws describing the relationship between the motion of a body and the forces acting on it, forming the foundation of classical mechanics. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion).
2.  The product of the mass and velocity of an object; a conserved vector quantity in a closed system. **Aliases:** linear momentum. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum).
3.  The quantitative property transferred to a body or physical system to perform work or to heat it; a conserved quantity. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy).
4.  A nonlinear partial differential equation describing the capillary pressure difference sustained across the interface between two static fluids, due to surface tension. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%E2%80%93Laplace_equation).

## Thermodynamics

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Laws of thermodynamics (1)
- Entropy (2)
- Leidenfrost effect (3)

</div>

1.  A set of physical laws defining physical quantities such as temperature, energy, and entropy that characterize thermodynamic systems in equilibrium. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_thermodynamics).
2.  A physical quantity, often described as a measure of a system's disorder, that quantifies the number of microscopic configurations consistent with a system's macroscopic state. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy).
3.  A phenomenon in which a liquid, close to a solid surface much hotter than the liquid's boiling point, produces an insulating vapor layer that keeps it from boiling rapidly. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leidenfrost_effect).

## Electromagnetism

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Maxwell's equations (1)
- Electromagnetic radiation (2)

</div>

1.  A set of coupled partial differential equations that, together with the Lorentz force law, form the foundation of classical electromagnetism, optics, and electric circuits. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%27s_equations).
2.  Waves of the electromagnetic field, propagating through space and carrying electromagnetic radiant energy — including radio waves, light, and X-rays. **Aliases:** EM radiation. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation).

## Waves & Optics

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Diffraction (1)
- Young's experiment (2)
- Bragg's law (3)

</div>

1.  The bending and interference of waves around obstacles or through apertures, characteristic of all wave phenomena. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction).
2.  An experiment demonstrating the wave-like nature of light through the interference pattern produced by light passing through two closely spaced slits. **Aliases:** double-slit experiment. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-slit_experiment).
3.  A physical law giving the angles for coherent scattering of waves from a crystal lattice, central to X-ray crystallography. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law).

## Quantum Mechanics

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Wave function (1)
- Uncertainty principle (2)
- Quantum entanglement (3)
- Quantum tunnelling (4)
- Josephson effect (5)
- Quantum computing (6)
- Qubit (7)

</div>

1.  A mathematical description of the quantum state of a system; its squared magnitude gives the probability of finding a particle in a given state. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function).
2.  A fundamental limit stating that the position and momentum of a particle cannot both be measured with arbitrary precision at the same time. **Aliases:** Heisenberg uncertainty principle. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_principle).
3.  A phenomenon in which the quantum states of two or more particles are correlated such that the state of each cannot be described independently of the others. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_entanglement).
4.  The quantum-mechanical phenomenon in which a wavefunction can propagate through a potential barrier that it could not surmount classically. **Aliases:** quantum tunneling. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunnelling).
5.  A phenomenon in which a supercurrent flows across two superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect).
6.  A type of computation that harnesses quantum states — superposition, interference, and entanglement — to perform calculations. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computing).
7.  The basic unit of quantum information — the quantum analogue of the classical bit, physically realized with a two-state device. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qubit).

## Relativity

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Special relativity (1)
- General relativity (2)
- Mass–energy equivalence (3)

</div>

1.  A theory describing the relationship between space and time for objects moving at constant speed, based on the constancy of the speed of light for all observers. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity).
2.  Einstein's geometric theory of gravitation, describing gravity as a curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity).
3.  The principle that mass and energy are interchangeable, expressed by the equation *E = mc²*. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence).

## Particle Physics

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Standard Model (1)
- Elementary particles (2)
- Higgs boson (3)

</div>

1.  The theory describing three of the four known fundamental forces and classifying all known elementary particles. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model).
2.  Particles with no known substructure — thus not known to be composed of other particles — such as quarks, leptons, and bosons. **Aliases:** elementary particle, fundamental particle. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle).
3.  An elementary particle in the Standard Model, produced by the quantum excitation of the Higgs field, which gives mass to other elementary particles. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson).
