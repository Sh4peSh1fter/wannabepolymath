# Key Terms & Concepts — Sociology

A curated, scannable reference of important terms in sociology. Hover a term to reveal its
definition, aliases, and an external anchor. Terms are grouped by real sub-fields — never a
`General` or `Other` catch-all.

## Social Theory

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Structural functionalism (1)
- Conflict theory (2)
- Symbolic interactionism (3)
- Social constructionism (4)
- Sociological imagination (5)

</div>

1.  A framework that views society as a complex system whose parts work together to promote solidarity and stability. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_functionalism).
2.  A family of perspectives that emphasize social conflict arising from inequality in power, status, or material resources. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_theories).
3.  A micro-level theory holding that people act toward things on the basis of meanings created and revised through social interaction. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_interactionism).
4.  The theory that many features of the social world — categories, knowledge, and institutions — are produced and sustained by collective human activity rather than being natural givens. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_constructionism).
5.  The capacity to connect individual experiences to larger social and historical forces, coined by C. Wright Mills. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociological_imagination).

## Social Structure & Institutions

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Social structure (1)
- Institution (2)
- Role (3)
- Bureaucracy (4)
- Mechanical and organic solidarity (5)

</div>

1.  The patterned and relatively enduring social arrangements — relationships, groups, and institutions — that shape and constrain individual behavior. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_structure).
2.  A stable, established structure of social order governing the behavior of a set of individuals within a community, such as the family, education, or religion. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution).
3.  The set of behaviors, rights, obligations, and expectations attached to a particular social position. **Aliases:** social role. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role).
4.  A form of organization characterized by hierarchy, formal rules, and impersonal, rationalized administration, analyzed influentially by Max Weber. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy).
5.  Émile Durkheim's distinction between social cohesion based on the similarity of members (mechanical) and cohesion based on interdependence through the division of labor (organic). **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_and_organic_solidarity).

## Stratification & Inequality

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Social stratification (1)
- Social class (2)
- Social mobility (3)
- Cultural capital (4)
- Intersectionality (5)

</div>

1.  The hierarchical arrangement of people in a society into layers or strata based on unequal access to resources, power, and prestige. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stratification).
2.  A grouping of people into hierarchical categories based on economic and social position. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_class).
3.  The movement of individuals or groups between positions in a system of social stratification. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_mobility).
4.  Non-financial social assets — such as education, tastes, and manners — that promote social mobility, a concept developed by Pierre Bourdieu. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_capital).
5.  The analytical framework, associated with Kimberlé Crenshaw, for understanding how overlapping social identities (such as race, gender, and class) combine to produce distinct forms of privilege or disadvantage. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersectionality).

## Deviance & Social Control

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Deviance (1)
- Social control (2)
- Labeling theory (3)
- Anomie (4)

</div>

1.  Behavior that violates the norms or expectations of a social group. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deviance_(sociology)).
2.  The mechanisms — formal and informal — by which a society regulates behavior and encourages conformity to its norms. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_control).
3.  The view that deviance is not inherent in an act but results from the labels society applies to people and behaviors. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labeling_theory).
4.  A condition of breakdown or weakening of social norms and values, introduced into sociology by Émile Durkheim. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomie).

## Culture & Socialization

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Culture (1)
- Socialization (2)
- Social norm (3)
- Ethnocentrism (4)
- Cultural relativism (5)

</div>

1.  The shared beliefs, values, practices, symbols, and material objects that characterize a group or society. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture).
2.  The lifelong process through which individuals internalize the norms, values, and behaviors of their society. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialization).
3.  A shared standard of acceptable behavior within a group or society. **Aliases:** norm. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_norm).
4.  The tendency to judge another culture by the standards of one's own, often assuming one's own to be superior. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnocentrism).
5.  The principle that a culture's beliefs and practices should be understood on their own terms rather than judged against another culture's standards. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_relativism).

## Research Methods

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Quantitative research (1)
- Qualitative research (2)
- Ethnography (3)
- Ideal type (4)

</div>

1.  Research that gathers and analyzes numerical data to identify patterns, test hypotheses, and generalize findings. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_research).
2.  Research that gathers non-numerical data — interviews, observations, texts — to explore meanings and social processes in depth. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_research).
3.  The systematic study of people and cultures through immersive, first-hand fieldwork. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnography).
4.  An analytical construct — associated with Max Weber — that abstracts the essential features of a phenomenon to serve as a benchmark for comparison, not a description of reality. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_type).
