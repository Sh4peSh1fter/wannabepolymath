# Key Terms & Concepts — Literature

A curated, scannable reference of important terms in literature. Hover a term to reveal its
definition, aliases, and an external anchor. Terms are grouped by real sub-topics — never a
`General` or `Other` catch-all.

## Literary Genres

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Fiction (1)
- Nonfiction (2)
- Novel (3)
- Short story (4)
- Poetry (5)
- Drama (6)
- Tragedy (7)
- Comedy (8)

</div>

1.  Narrative literature describing imaginary events and people rather than factual record. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiction).
2.  Prose writing that presents information, argument, or narrative offered as factual. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonfiction).
3.  An extended work of narrative fiction, typically written in prose and published as a book. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel).
4.  A short work of prose fiction, usually focused on a single incident or effect. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story).
5.  Literature that uses the aesthetic and often rhythmic qualities of language — such as meter and rhyme — to evoke meaning beyond the literal. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry).
6.  A genre of literature composed for performance, in which a story is told through the dialogue and action of characters. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama).
7.  A dramatic genre depicting the downfall of a protagonist, often through a fatal flaw or inescapable circumstance. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy).
8.  A genre intended to be humorous or to end happily, often satirizing its subjects. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy).

## Narrative & Poetics

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Narrative (1)
- Plot (2)
- Narration (3)
- Character (4)
- Setting (5)
- Theme (6)
- Poetics (7)
- Stream of consciousness (8)

</div>

1.  An account of connected events, real or imagined, presented to a reader or listener. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative).
2.  The arrangement of events in a narrative, including their causal and temporal relationships. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plot_(narrative)).
3.  The act of telling a story, including the voice, perspective, and point of view through which it is conveyed. **Aliases:** point of view. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narration).
4.  A person, animal, or being who takes part in the action of a narrative. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_(arts)).
5.  The time and place in which the events of a narrative occur. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setting_(narrative)).
6.  The central idea, message, or underlying meaning explored in a literary work. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_(narrative)).
7.  The theory and study of literary form and discourse — how texts are constructed and achieve their effects. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics).
8.  A narrative mode that renders the continuous, unfiltered flow of a character's thoughts and impressions. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_of_consciousness).

## Literary Devices

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Metaphor (1)
- Simile (2)
- Allegory (3)
- Irony (4)
- Foreshadowing (5)
- Alliteration (6)
- Personification (7)
- Motif (8)

</div>

1.  A figure of speech that describes one thing as being another, asserting an implicit comparison. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor).
2.  A figure of speech directly comparing two unlike things, usually with "like" or "as." **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simile).
3.  A narrative in which characters and events symbolically represent abstract ideas or moral qualities. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory).
4.  A contrast between expectation and reality, or between what is said and what is meant. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irony).
5.  A device that hints at events to come later in a narrative. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreshadowing).
6.  The repetition of initial consonant sounds in neighboring words. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliteration).
7.  The attribution of human characteristics to non-human things or abstractions. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personification).
8.  A recurring image, idea, or element that reinforces a work's theme. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_(narrative)).

## Poetry & Prosody

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Meter (1)
- Rhyme (2)
- Stanza (3)
- Sonnet (4)
- Iambic pentameter (5)
- Free verse (6)
- Haiku (7)

</div>

1.  The rhythmic structure of a line of verse, measured in patterns of stressed and unstressed syllables. **Aliases:** metre. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(poetry)).
2.  The repetition of similar sounds, usually at the ends of lines of verse. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyme).
3.  A grouped set of lines within a poem, set off by a blank line. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanza).
4.  A fourteen-line poem in a fixed rhyme scheme, traditionally in iambic pentameter. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonnet).
5.  A poetic meter of five iambic feet per line, widely used in English verse and drama. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iambic_pentameter).
6.  Poetry that does not follow a regular meter or rhyme scheme. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_verse).
7.  A Japanese form of short poem, traditionally in three phrases of roughly five, seven, and five sound units. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiku).

## Literary Movements

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Romanticism (1)
- Literary realism (2)
- Naturalism (3)
- Modernism (4)
- Postmodern literature (5)
- Magic realism (6)

</div>

1.  A late-18th- and 19th-century movement emphasizing emotion, individualism, and the sublime in nature. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism).
2.  A movement seeking to represent everyday life and ordinary people truthfully, without idealization. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_realism).
3.  An outgrowth of realism applying scientific determinism to fiction, stressing environment and heredity. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(literature)).
4.  An early-20th-century movement marked by formal experimentation and a break with traditional narrative conventions. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_literature).
5.  A post-World War II tendency characterized by irony, fragmentation, metafiction, and skepticism toward grand narratives. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_literature).
6.  A style in which magical elements appear in an otherwise realistic setting, presented as ordinary. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_realism).

## Literary Criticism & Theory

<div class="annotate" markdown>

- Literary criticism (1)
- Literary theory (2)
- New Criticism (3)
- Structuralism (4)
- Deconstruction (5)
- Close reading (6)
- Western canon (7)

</div>

1.  The study, evaluation, and interpretation of literature. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_criticism).
2.  The systematic study of the nature of literature and of the methods for analyzing it. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_theory).
3.  A mid-20th-century movement that emphasized close reading of the text itself, apart from author biography or historical context. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Criticism).
4.  An approach analyzing literature as a system of interrelated signs and underlying structures, drawing on structural linguistics. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structuralism).
5.  A critical approach, associated with Jacques Derrida, that exposes the unstable and contradictory meanings within a text. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconstruction).
6.  The careful, sustained interpretation of a short passage of text, attending to detail and form. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_reading).
7.  The body of literary works traditionally regarded as most important and influential in Western culture. **Aliases:** literary canon. **See also:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_canon).
